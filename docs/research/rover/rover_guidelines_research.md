# Crewed rover driving: what NASA recommends

For: LARA driving mode. Habitat in Arcadia Planitia (~46 N, 184 E), science sites 90–500 km away, main risk = battery running low on bad terrain.
Date: 2026-11-15. Built from five research passes. A separate verifier re-checked every number at its source; the "At source?" column gives that verdict.

Reading key

- **Moon** = Apollo LRV or Artemis LTV. **Mars robotic** = Curiosity / Perseverance (JPL). **Mars crewed study** = a NASA design study. Its numbers are assumptions, not requirements.
- **derived** = our own arithmetic. No source states it. Do not cite it as NASA's.
- Row IDs (L1, W5, D21 …) are used by `proposed_rover_rules.md`.

---

## (a) Short answer

The only real operating rules NASA has for a crewed rover come from Apollo:

- The LRV could range no farther from the lander than the crew could walk back on their backpacks, about 9.4–9.7 km.
- It drove at up to 14 km/h and was rated for 25° slopes.
- Planners recomputed a walk-back envelope (rover fails) and a ride-back envelope (backpack fails) at every station.

For Mars there are no flight rules, only design studies: DRA 5.0 (2009), Exploration Zone / Evolvable Mars Campaign (2015–2018), the Mars Architecture Team power study (Chappell et al. 2022) and the Glenn COMPASS rover TM (2022). They agree on:

- a small pressurized rover with 2 crew and room for 4 in a rescue
- 1–2 week sorties
- about 100 km straight-line reach (about 400 km on the odometer)
- planning speeds of 3–8 km/h and about 5 h of daylight driving per sol
- a 30% allowance for route length beyond the planned path
- Li-ion batteries run to 70–80% depth of discharge
- a two-rover rule: one rover alone may go only as far as the crew could walk back (~15 km on Mars, per DRA 5.0); a second rover allows the full ~100 km zone, because either rover can bring everyone home.

The only NASA energy model for a crewed Mars rover is flat-ground rolling resistance, E = Crr·m·g·d with Crr = 0.15. That 0.15 is a lunar-soil value. The slope term W·sinθ comes from a separate NASA LTV terramechanics paper.

What does not exist for Mars:

- a slope limit for crewed driving (the usable numbers are JPL robotic ones: ~20° on firm ground, ~10° on sand)
- a battery-reserve flight rule (closest: one study's "always keep enough for a 20 km drive back", and Perseverance's robotic 40% minimum charge)
- any measured rolling-resistance coefficient for Mars soil
- any NASA guidance on the DEM resolution needed for crewed traverse planning
- any NASA concept that reaches sites 250–500 km away without supply caches or deployed power.

## (c) The energy model in the NASA/JPL literature

No NASA document gives one complete equation. The pieces, each from a NASA source:

| Piece                             | Form                                     | Source                                |
| --------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| Rolling resistance on flat ground | F_roll = Crr·m·g; E = Crr·m·g·d          | Chappell 2022 eqs 1–4 (C3)            |
| Gravity on a slope                | F_grav = m·g·sinθ (+ uphill, − downhill) | NASA LTV terramechanics paper (N1)    |
| Normalized energy                 | PN = E/(W·d)                             | NASA/TP-2017-219384 (N4)              |
| Apollo check                      | pull/weight ≈ tan(slope) at steady speed | TR R-401 (N6)                         |
| cosθ on the rolling term          | F = μ·m·g·cosθ + m·g·sinθ                | Only in non-NASA preprints (N13, N14) |

Put together, per km of path (our assembly):

```
F    = m · g · (k · Crr · cos θ + sin θ)                    [N]   θ signed along heading, + uphill
F    = max(F, 0)                                                  no regenerative braking credit
E_km = f_path · [ F / (3.6 · η · (1 − s))  +  P_hotel / v ]  [Wh/km]   P_hotel in W, v in km/h
```

How this maps to LARA (our reading). A driving plan has to satisfy three nested return checks at every node, as Apollo did per station:

1. **Energy drive-back.** Usable energy left must cover the return path, costed at bad-terrain rates, plus a reserve (C2).
2. **Suit walk-back (one rover).** If the rover dies here, can the crew walk to a safe haven on their suits (D21, W12)?
3. **Buddy rover (two rovers).** Beyond walk-back, a second rover must be present, able to seat 4 and to drive both crews home (E9, D24).

Derived check: the team's suit rule gives (480 − 30) / 1.3 = 346 usable minutes. At Apollo 17's 2.7 km/h contingency pace (W12), that is 15.6 km, which matches DRA 5.0's ~15 km (D21). Tobler's function on flat ground gives ~5 km/h, which would allow ~29 km. That is much more than Apollo assumed for a tired, suited crew walking home.

Inference, not stated in any source: with almost no HRSC in the box, the 200 m blended DEM over Arcadia is essentially resampled MOLA, so it adds no real detail.

What resolution means for slope hazards:

- A slope is only as fine as its baseline, which is at least 2 DEM cells (T27). MOLA slopes describe ~0.5–1 km of ground.
- Coarse DEMs read slopes too low. HiRISE vs MOLA differ by 6.37° on average, and HiRISE vs CTX by 5.32° (T27). In the northern plains, MOLA-scale slopes are typically <0.5° (T24). **A 20° no-go rule will almost never trigger on MOLA data in Arcadia.** The hazards that matter are below the cell size: crater rims, scarps, terraces, ripples and rocks.
- At meter scale the plains are mostly benign. Phoenix-region slopes were 1.8–2.8° at 1 m, and >16° was "extremely rare" (T28). Starship-site HiRISE DEMs met <5° over 10 m (T31). So hazards are discrete features, not the general surface.
- Fine DEMs have the opposite problem. A 3 m height error reads as a 9° slope over 20 m but <1° over 200 m (T16). Slopes from CTX need smoothing or a check against noise.
- NASA/JPL precedent for scale-dependent limits exists, but only for landing: MSL ≤3° over 2–5 km, ≤5° over 200–500 m, ≤15° over 20–40 m (R29); MER <5° at 100 m, <2° at 1.2 km (T29). Mars 2020 traversability used HiRISE DEMs plus rock maps (R5, R6).
- Rocks. HiRISE resolves rocks ≥~1.5 m (R5). Rover obstacle limits are 0.3–0.5 m (L18, R26, R43), so no orbital product maps obstacles at the scale a rover cares about. Rock cover (CFA) is the only available proxy.
- Better data near the plan. CTX DTMs cluster at ~46 N, 188.5–189 E and 46–47 N, 193–195 E (T17). The first cluster sits on the NASA Ames proposed landing site at 46.16 N, 188.8 E (E20), about 200 km east of the habitat (derived). A HiRISE DTM covers Badger crater at 46.56 N, 194.86 E (T21, T34), about 450 km east (derived).
- Latitude. 46 N is inside the EZ limit of ±50° (E3) but near the 50–60° band where MSL expected efficiency losses from cold, frost and low sun (R31). The Starship study required <40° (T30).
- Ground ice. Arcadia has ice within metres of the surface: 0.15 m at the Ames site (E20), and see T33–T36. No source found says how ice-rich ground affects trafficability; we did not search for this specifically.

NASA has no rule on DEM resolution for crewed traverse planning. Its EZ papers say traversability will be "assessed separately" (E17).
